When a band reaches a certain level of global saturation, their most famous songs can obscure the actual mechanics of what makes them interesting. Radiohead is arguably the defining alternative rock band of the past thirty years, but if you only know "Creep" and "Karma Police," you are missing the rhythmic complexity and careful tension that characterizes their best work. 

To explore their catalog beyond the stadium anthems, we are focusing on three tracks that highlight their mastery of arrangement and structure.

**"Let Down" (*OK Computer*, 1997)**
While "Paranoid Android" is the widely acknowledged masterpiece of *OK Computer*, "Let Down" is the album’s structural marvel. The song is built on a polyrhythm. Jonny Greenwood plays a guitar arpeggio in a 5/4 time signature, while the rest of the band plays in standard 4/4. This creates a subtle, mathematical tension; the guitar line constantly falls out of sync with the drums and bass, only to resolve and lock back in at the end of the musical phrase. It gives the song a feeling of perpetual forward motion that mirrors the lyrical themes of transit and isolation.

**"Weird Fishes/Arpeggi" (*In Rainbows*, 2007)**
By 2007, Radiohead had fully integrated electronic production techniques into their live instrumentation. "Weird Fishes/Arpeggi" is essentially electronic music played by a rock band. The foundation of the track relies on three intertwining, cyclical guitar riffs (the "arpeggi" of the title). Instead of a traditional verse-chorus structure, the song functions more like a DJ set building to a crescendo. The rhythm section, driven by Phil Selway’s motorik drum pattern, remains tightly constrained until the final two minutes, when the tension finally breaks into a sweeping, oceanic release.

**"The Daily Mail" (*The King of Limbs: Live from the Basement*, 2011)**
Released as a standalone single recorded during a live studio session, "The Daily Mail" demonstrates how effectively the band can manipulate dynamics. It opens as a quiet, solitary piano ballad. There is no indication of where the song is heading until the two-minute mark, when the entire band enters simultaneously alongside a brass section. The arrangement shifts instantly from melancholic isolation to aggressive, chaotic rock. It is a masterclass in holding a listener's attention through restraint before delivering a massive, earned payoff.

Radiohead's discography demands active listening. The next time you search for them, bypass the top five most played tracks and listen to how carefully they construct the space between the notes.
